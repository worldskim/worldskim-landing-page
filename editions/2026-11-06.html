A 200 Year Sentence Leaves Wider Questions About Shakahola

A Kenyan court has sentenced Paul Mackenzie to 200 years for murdering 191 people, all but one children. The judge assigned 100 years for homicide and another 100 for starvation offences, with the categories running consecutively. Prosecutors sought execution, but the judge declined to impose the death penalty.

The sentence covers 191 victims, while more than 400 bodies were discovered in Shakahola forest. Mackenzie faces separate manslaughter and terrorism charges, which he denies. Beyond his prosecution, Kenya's human rights commission found that local officials repeatedly ignored the cult's activities. A presidential inquiry into the deaths was later declared unconstitutional.

Tanzania Clears Lissu of Treason but Keeps Him Behind Bars

Tanzanian opposition leader Tundu Lissu was acquitted of treason on Friday, but convicted of incitement and sentenced to four years. His party's spokesperson told Reuters that he plans to appeal. Prosecutors based the case on a speech they said urged supporters to block an election and mobilise rebellion.

Lissu says his speech broke no law and the charges were politically motivated. The government denies suppressing dissent and says protesters started the violence surrounding the October 2025 election. A presidential commission reported at least 518 deaths in that unrest. Rights groups accused security services of excessive force, which the government denied.

Federal Approval Does Not Settle Whether Trump's Arch Can Be Built

A federal planning commission approved President Trump's proposed arch by 8 to 4, but construction cannot begin. Judge Tanya Chutkan ordered a 14 day pause after requests from Washington, D.C., and Virginia. The proposed 250 foot structure would occupy Memorial Circle, between the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington National Cemetery.

The dispute separates planning approval from the legal authority to construct a monument. Veterans and local governments argue that congressional permission is required, but the administration has not sought it. Chutkan also agreed that traffic disruption would cause immediate harm. Her temporary order does not finally decide whether the arch is lawful.

Pope Leo's Latin American Journey Begins in Its Most Secular Country

Pope Leo XIV departed Rome on Friday morning for Uruguay, Argentina and Peru, beginning a journey scheduled through Nov. 17. He is due in Montevideo later Friday, according to the Vatican's published schedule. The trip begins three weeks after lung cancer surgery, with medical care accompanying the 71 year old pontiff.

Latin America is home to around 40% of the world's Catholics, but Uruguay has the region's strongest secular identity. It recently approved assisted dying legislation despite Catholic opposition. The visit is Uruguay's first by a pope since 1988. Leo is expected to discuss human dignity while restating church opposition to abortion and euthanasia.

Uruguayan church figure Guzmán Carriquiry believes the visit could renew interest in Catholic traditions. Scholar Andrew Chesnut expects a warm reception, but doubts enthusiasm will translate into church attendance. Separately, Vatican communications chief Maria Montserrat Alvar says doctors are confident about Leo's recovery. She says he has completed the 14 day convalescence period required after his Oct. 13 operation.

The published programme includes meetings with Uruguay's authorities, followed by travel to Argentina and Peru. Vatican News lists visits to 10 cities and approximately 12 flights across the journey. Peru carries particular personal significance: Leo served there as a missionary and bishop for two decades. The itinerary includes Lima, Chiclayo, Cusco and Pucallpa, according to Vatican News.
